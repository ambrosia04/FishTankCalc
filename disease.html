Ich / White Spot Disease

Mortality Rate: Moderate to High if untreated.

Symptoms

  • White dots on fins and body
  • Flashing against surfaces
  • Rapid breathing
  • Lethargy

Quarantine

  • Quarantine the entire tank because Ich spreads rapidly.
  • Remove sensitive species if medication is dangerous for them.

Treatment

  • Increase temperature gradually
  • Use Ich medication
  • Add aquarium salt in hospital tank (2 tablespoons per gallon of water)
  • Improve water quality

Saddleback Disease

Mortality Rate: High if untreated.

What Is It?

Saddleback Disease is usually a form of advanced Columnaris bacterial infection. It creates a pale or gray “saddle” patch across the fish’s back, often near the dorsal fin.

Symptoms

  • White, gray, or pale patch across the back
  • Cotton-like lesions
  • Rapid breathing
  • Fin deterioration
  • Lethargy
  • Loss of appetite

Quarantine

  • Quarantine the infected fish immediately.
  • If multiple fish show symptoms, treat the entire aquarium.
  • Disinfect nets and equipment after contact.

Treatment

  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics
  • Lower temperature slightly (Columnaris worsens in heat)
  • Increase oxygenation
  • Daily water changes
  • Remove decaying organic waste

Prevention

  • Maintain stable water parameters
  • Avoid overcrowding
  • Reduce stress during transport/acclimation
  • Quarantine new fish before introduction

Fish Tuberculosis (Mycobacteriosis)

Mortality Rate: Extremely High / Terminal.
⚠️ HUMAN RISK: This can cause "Fish Tank Granuloma" in humans. Always wear gloves if you have open cuts on your hands.

Symptoms

  • Wasting Away: The fish looks thin or has a sunken belly despite eating.
  • Spinal Deformities: Sudden bending or "humpback" appearance (Scoliosis).
  • Skin Ulcers: Open sores or loss of scales that do not heal with standard treatments.
  • Lethargy: Fish stays at the bottom or hides constantly.
  • Popeye: One or both eyes bulging out.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Immediate Isolation: Remove the fish at the first sign of wasting.
  • Sterilization: Do NOT share nets, siphons, or tools between a suspected TB tank and other tanks.
  • Do Not Feed Cull Fish: Never feed dead fish to other tank mates.

Treatment

  • No Reliable Cure: Most medications only mask symptoms. The bacteria (Mycobacterium) are incredibly resistant.
  • Euthanasia: Generally considered the most humane option to prevent the slow suffering of the fish and the spread of the colony.
  • UV Sterilizers: Can help reduce the number of free-floating bacteria in the water column.

Slime Coat Syndrome / Slime Disease

Mortality Rate: Moderate to High if untreated (often due to respiratory failure or secondary infections).

What Is It?

Slime Coat Syndrome is not a single disease, but rather a defensive physiological response. When a fish's skin is irritated by external parasites, poor water chemistry, or harsh chemicals, its mucus-producing cells go into overdrive. This excess mucus sloughs off the body, giving the fish a cloudy, hazy, or peeling appearance.

Symptoms

  • Cloudy or Milky Film: A gray or white mucus coating covering the body, fins, or eyes.
  • Peeling Slime: Strands of mucus peeling and trailing off the fish's body.
  • Flashing & Scratching: Fish scraping themselves against decorations or substrate.
  • Clamped Fins & Lethargy: Fins held close to the body, accompanied by sluggish movement.
  • Rapid Gilling: Labored breathing or gasping at the surface as mucus builds up on the gill membranes.

Common Causes

  • Parasites: Microscopic protozoans like Chilodonella, Ichthyobodo (Costia), or Trichodina.
  • Water Quality Issues: Spikes in ammonia or nitrite, chlorine exposure, or sudden pH fluctuations (pH shock).
  • Physical Injury: Rough handling, aggressive tankmates, or abrasive decor that strips away the protective slime layer.

Quarantine

  • Isolate affected fish to a dedicated hospital tank if only one or two individuals show symptoms.
  • If the issue stems from tank-wide parasites or poor water quality, address and treat the main aquarium environment.

Treatment

  • Optimize Water Quality: Immediately test water parameters and conduct gradual, temperature-matched water changes using a high-quality water conditioner.
  • Aquarium Salt: Adding 1 tablespoon of aquarium salt per 3 gallons of water helps restore essential electrolytes and stimulates natural slime coat regeneration.
  • Chemical Treatments: If protozoan parasites are suspected, utilize medications containing formalin, malachite green, or copper sulfate.
  • Supportive Additives: Introduce stress-reducing water additives (such as products containing aloe vera or Indian almond leaves) to soothe irritated tissue.

Epistylis

Mortality Rate: Moderate to Very High.

What Is It?

Epistylis is a protozoan infection commonly mistaken for Ich. Unlike Ich, the white spots are usually raised and may appear fuzzy. It often appears secondary to poor water quality or bacterial infections.

Symptoms

  • Raised white lesions
  • Fuzzy white patches
  • Red irritated skin
  • Rapid breathing
  • Lethargy
  • Loss of appetite

Quarantine

  • Quarantine infected fish immediately.
  • If several fish show symptoms, treat the whole aquarium.
  • Improve water quality urgently.

Treatment

  • Broad-spectrum antibiotics
  • Improve water quality
  • Increase aeration
  • Reduce organic waste
  • Hospital tank recommended

Important Note

Unlike Ich, increasing temperature can worsen Epistylis. Correct identification is extremely important. Check the Useful Links for a comparison of Epistylis vs Ich.

Neon Tetra Disease

Mortality Rate: Extremely High (Often 100%).

What Is It?

Neon Tetra Disease (NTD) is a highly contagious and usually fatal parasitic disease primarily affecting characins, but can also infect other fish species. It's caused by the microsporidian parasite Pleistophora hyphessobryconis. There is no known cure, and it's best to act quickly to prevent spread.

Symptoms

  • Pale/Discolored Patches: Loss of color, especially in the neon stripe.
  • Wasting Away: Fish becomes emaciated despite eating (often mistaken for Fish TB).
  • Lumps or Cysts: May appear on the body.
  • Spinal Deformities: Curved spine as the disease progresses.
  • Erratic Swimming: Jerky movements or difficulty swimming.
  • Secondary Infections: Fungus or bacterial infections may develop on weakened fish.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Immediate Euthanasia: Unfortunately, the best course of action for an infected fish is humane euthanasia to prevent spread.
  • Remove Carcasses: The parasite spreads when healthy fish eat infected dead fish or their waste.
  • Disinfect: Do NOT share equipment (nets, siphons) between tanks. Sterilize everything that has come into contact with the infected tank.
  • Quarantine New Fish: Always quarantine new fish for 4-6 weeks to prevent introducing the disease.

Treatment

  • No Cure: There is no effective treatment for Neon Tetra Disease once established.
  • Supportive Care: Keeping water quality pristine and reducing stress can sometimes prolong life but will not cure the disease.
  • Euthanasia is the most humane option for infected individuals.

Black Spot Disease / Black Ich

Mortality Rate: Low (usually not fatal, but highly irritating).

What Is It?

Black Spot Disease (also known as Black Ich or Black Grub) is caused by the larval stage (metacercariae) of parasitic trematodes (flukes) of the genus Posthodiplostomum or Apophallus. The black spots are actually the fish's melanistic (dark pigment) reaction to wall off the parasite under the skin. It typically enters the aquarium through live food or wild-caught fish.
It can sometimes get confused with necrotic or healed injuries.

Symptoms

  • Small Black Dots: Visible tiny black dots (like pepper flakes) scattered on the body, fins, and sometimes eyes.
  • Flashing/Scratching: Fish will rub against objects to try and dislodge the parasites.
  • Lethargy: May appear stressed or less active in severe infestations.
  • Reduced Appetite: In rare, heavy infestations.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Quarantine New Fish: Always quarantine new fish for at least 4 weeks.
  • Avoid Wild-Caught Food: Do not feed live foods collected from the wild that could carry parasites.
  • Snail Control: The parasite's life cycle often involves snails as an intermediate host. Removing snails can break the cycle.

Treatment

  • Praziquantel: Medications containing praziquantel (e.g., API General Cure) are effective.
  • Salt Baths: Short-term salt baths can help dislodge some parasites and alleviate irritation.
  • Copper-Based Medications: Can be effective, but use with caution and avoid with invertebrates.
  • Improve Water Quality: Maintaining pristine water conditions helps fish recover and reduces stress.

Duck Lips Disease

Mortality Rate: Moderate to High if untreated.

What Is It?

"Duck Lips Disease" is a common term for a fungal infection, often caused by Saprolegnia or similar water molds, that specifically affects the mouth region of fish. It can also be a secondary bacterial infection. It is characterized by swollen, often white or fuzzy, lesions around the mouth, making it difficult for the fish to eat or breathe properly.
It can be confused with other mouth deformities like Lip Fibromas or other injuries, so proper diagnosis is important.

Symptoms

  • Swollen Lips/Mouth: The mouth area appears enlarged, puffy, or protrudes.
  • White or Fuzzy Growths: Cotton-like patches around the mouth and jaw.
  • Difficulty Eating: Fish may refuse food or struggle to pick it up.
  • Labored Breathing: Rapid gill movements if the infection spreads.
  • Lethargy: Reduced activity and hiding.
  • Erosion: In advanced stages, mouth tissue may erode.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Isolate Infected Fish: Move the affected fish to a hospital tank immediately.
  • Improve Water Quality: Poor water conditions are a primary trigger. Perform water changes in the main tank.
  • Reduce Stress: Ensure stable parameters, appropriate tankmates, and proper diet.
  • Avoid Injury: Ensure no sharp decorations or aggressive tankmates cause mouth injuries.

Treatment

  • Antifungal Medication: Use a broad-spectrum antifungal specifically designed for aquarium fish.
  • Antibacterial Medication: If secondary bacterial infection is suspected, combine with an appropriate antibiotic.
  • Aquarium Salt: Add 1-2 tablespoons per 5 gallons in the hospital tank to aid healing and reduce fungal growth.
  • Clean Water: Maintain immaculate water quality in the hospital tank with daily small water changes.
  • Medicated Food: If the fish is still eating, medicated food can be highly effective.

Chilodonella

Mortality Rate: Very High (especially in weakened fish or crowded tanks).

What Is It?

Chilodonella is a highly pathogenic, ciliated protozoan parasite that infects the skin and gills of freshwater fish. It causes severe irritation and can quickly lead to suffocation if the gills are heavily infested. It thrives in poor water quality and cooler temperatures.

Symptoms

  • Cloudy or Milky Skin: A bluish-grey film or slime coat on the body.
  • Flashing/Rubbing: Fish constantly scrape themselves against decorations or substrate.
  • Clamped Fins: Fins held close to the body.
  • Rapid Breathing/Gasping: If gills are affected, fish may gasp at the surface.
  • Lethargy: Fish become listless and may hide or stay at the bottom.
  • Loss of Appetite: Refusal to eat.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Treat Entire Tank: Chilodonella is highly contagious and spreads rapidly, requiring whole-tank treatment.
  • Improve Water Quality: Maintain pristine water parameters and good oxygenation.
  • Avoid Overcrowding: Reduces stress and spread of parasites.
  • Quarantine New Fish: Isolate new fish for at least 4 weeks.

Treatment

  • Increase Temperature: Gradually raise the water temperature to 82-86°F (28-30°C) to speed up the parasite's life cycle, making it more vulnerable to medication.
  • Aquarium Salt: 2-3 tablespoons per 5 gallons can be effective, especially in a hospital tank.
  • Formalin-based medications: Highly effective against Chilodonella. Follow product instructions carefully.
  • Malachite Green: Also very effective, often found in combination with formalin.
  • Frequent Water Changes: Essential for removing free-swimming parasites.

Alkalosis (High pH Stress)

Mortality Rate: Moderate to High (if not corrected quickly).

What Is It?

Alkalosis is a condition in fish caused by exposure to excessively high pH levels in the aquarium water. While some fish prefer alkaline water, extreme or sudden spikes in pH can burn gills, damage the slime coat, and interfere with vital bodily functions, leading to severe stress, disease, and potentially death.

Symptoms

  • Rapid Gilling: Fish breathing heavily, often gasping at the surface.
  • Clamped Fins: Fins held close to the body, indicating severe stress.
  • Slime Coat Issues: Cloudy eyes, excess slime production, or visible damage to the slime coat.
  • Faded Colors: Fish lose their vibrant coloration.
  • Lethargy/Erratic Swimming: Listlessness, hiding, or swimming erratically.
  • Fin/Skin Erosion: In severe cases, fins and skin may show signs of chemical burns.
  • Death: Sudden, unexplained deaths.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Test pH Regularly: Use a reliable pH test kit. Know the ideal pH range for your specific fish species.
  • Buffer Water Appropriately: Use appropriate buffers or substrates (like crushed coral for high pH, Indian almond leaves for low pH) to maintain stable pH.
  • Avoid Sudden Changes: Never make drastic pH adjustments. Any changes should be slow and gradual over hours or days.
  • Check Tap Water: Test your tap water's pH before adding it to the tank during water changes.

Treatment

  • Gradual Water Changes: Perform small, frequent water changes (10-15% daily) with properly conditioned water that matches the tank's *target* pH, not necessarily the current high pH. The goal is to slowly bring the pH down.
  • Increase Aeration: High pH can make ammonia more toxic; increased oxygenation helps reduce stress.
  • Indian Almond Leaves/Alder Cones: These release tannins that can naturally and gently lower pH over time, and provide soothing properties.
  • pH Lowering Products: Use commercial pH lowering products ONLY if you understand how to use them safely and gradually, as sudden drops can be more harmful than high pH.
  • Monitor Fish Closely: Observe for signs of recovery or worsening condition.

Cladogonium

Mortality Rate: Moderate to High.

Symptoms

  • Green fuzzy growth
  • Failed molts
  • Egg infections

Quarantine

  • Quarantine infected shrimp immediately. It can be a small container.
  • If multiple shrimp are infected, quarantine the whole colony.

Treatment

  • 1. Prepare a 3% hydrogen peroxide solution or a 20% aquarium salt solution in a separate container.
  • 2. Gently dip the infected shrimp into the solution for 30-60 seconds.
  • 3. Remove the shrimp and rinse it in clean tank water before returning it to the quarantine tank.
  • 4. Repeat the dip every day until the infection is cleared (normally within a week).

Important Note

Cladogonium is a stubborn algae that can be difficult to eradicate. It often requires multiple treatments and strict quarantine measures. Always remove molts immediately, as the algae can cling to the old shell and reinfect the shrimp.
An airator can help keep the shrimp healthy during treatment by improving oxygen levels in the quarantine tank.
If not available, using a cup to take the water and letting it fall from a height oxygenates it.

When it's green, it's in the initial stages and more treatable. It will then turn pale green, yellowish and finally white or orange. The shrimp is recovered once it's fallen off and the new shell after a molt is clear of algae.

Scutariella Japonica

Mortality Rate: Low, but highly annoying and can stress the host.

Symptoms

  • Small white "finger-like" parasites on the head (rostrum) or gills.
  • Visible white dots in the gill chamber (these are eggs).
  • Shrimp may pick at their face or act irritated.

Quarantine

  • Isolate infected shrimp for treatment.
  • Critical: Remove molts from the tank immediately for 3 weeks, as the eggs stay in the old shell to hatch.

Treatment

  • Salt Dips: Extremely effective. Use 1 tablespoon of aquarium salt in 1 cup of tank water; dip the shrimp for 30-60 seconds. The worms usually fall off instantly.
  • Praziquantel (API General Cure): Effective for whole-tank treatment if many shrimp are affected.

Planaria (Flatworms)

Mortality Rate: High for shrimplets and snails; Moderate for adults.

Symptoms

  • Flat, gliding worms on the glass or substrate.
  • Identification: Look for a distinct arrow-shaped head and two eyespots.
  • Shrimp deaths with no visible external disease (Planaria hunt by paralyzing shrimp).

Quarantine

  • Planaria live in the substrate; if you see one, the entire tank is likely infested.
  • Reduce feeding immediately; Planaria thrive on leftover organic waste.

Treatment

  • Planaria Traps: Good for reducing numbers without chemicals.
  • Genchem "No Planaria" (Betel Nut Extract): Shrimp safe, but will kill most snails.
  • Panacur-C (Fenbendazole): Very effective at small doses (0.1g per 10 gallons), but kills Nerite and Mystery snails.

Muscular Necrosis

Mortality Rate: High (Once the white area reaches the head, it is terminal).

What Is It?

Muscular Necrosis is the degeneration of muscle fibers. The tissue dies and turns opaque, starting at the tail and moving toward the head. It is often caused by extreme stress, such as sudden temperature swings, low oxygen levels, or very poor water quality, but it can also be a secondary symptom of a bacterial infection.

Symptoms

  • Milky White Tail: The tail muscle becomes opaque and white (looking like a cooked shrimp).
  • Progressive Whitening: The white area grows larger over several days.
  • Lethargy: Shrimp stops swimming and remains stationary.
  • Loss of Appetite: The shrimp stops scavenging.

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Isolate Immediately: While not always contagious, other shrimp will eat a dying necrotic shrimp and potentially contract the bacteria that caused the stress.
  • Check Parameters: Immediately test for Ammonia/Nitrite and check your heater/thermometer for malfunctions.
  • Increase Aeration: Ensure there is plenty of surface agitation; low oxygen is a leading cause of necrosis.

Treatment

  • No Direct Cure: Once muscle tissue is necrotic (dead), it cannot be "healed."
  • Fix the Environment: If the necrosis is caught very early (a tiny white tip on the tail), a large water change and reducing stress can sometimes stop the progression.
  • Tannins: Add Indian Almond Leaves or Alder Cones to help fight potential bacterial causes.
  • Euthanasia: If the white area covers more than half the body, the shrimp will likely die; euthanasia is recommended to prevent them from being eaten by tank mates.

White Ring of Death (Failed Molt)

Mortality Rate: Very High (Usually fatal within 24 hours).

What Is It?

The "White Ring of Death" occurs when a shrimp's exoskeleton splits in preparation for molting, but the shrimp is unable to pull its body out of the old shell. The white band you see is actually the gap where the old shell has separated, exposing the soft new shell underneath.

Symptoms

  • The White Ring: A clear, thick white band appearing around the "neck" area (the junction between the carapace and the abdomen).
  • Lethargy: The shrimp stops moving or scavenging.
  • Attempted "Flicking": The shrimp may sporadically jump or flick its tail violently trying to escape the shell.

Common Causes

  • Improper Hardness: General Hardness (GH) or Carbonate Hardness (KH) being too high (shell is too hard to break) or too low (shell is too soft to provide leverage).
  • Osmotic Shock: Doing a large water change with water that has a vastly different TDS (Total Dissolved Solids) can trigger a "premature molt" before the shrimp is ready.
  • Dietary Deficiency: Lack of essential minerals like Calcium and Magnesium.
  • Overfeeding: High-protein diets can cause the shrimp's body to grow faster than its shell can keep up with.

Prevention & Treatment

  • Stability is Key: Use a TDS meter and ensure your water changes are done slowly (drip method is best).
  • Check Parameters: Aim for GH 6-8 and KH 2-5 for Neocaridina; GH 4-6 and KH 0-1 for Caridina.
  • Mineral Supplements: Provide mineral stones or specialized shrimp foods containing calcium.
  • Note on Treatment: Do NOT attempt to manually peel the shell off. Shrimp are incredibly delicate; attempting to "help" them will almost always result in crushing them or causing fatal stress. Stable water is the only chance they have to finish the molt on their own.

Rust Disease (Bacterial Shell Disease)

Mortality Rate: Moderate (Fatal if it penetrates the body).

What Is It?

Caused by "chitinolytic" bacteria that produce an enzyme to break down the shrimp’s exoskeleton. It usually starts at injury sites or during periods of poor water quality. It is often called Rust Disease because the shrimp's immune response creates a brown, "rusty" pigment at the site of the infection.

Symptoms

  • Rusty-brown, orange, or black pitted spots on the shell.
  • Visible erosion or "holes" in the carapace or tail.
  • Loss of appendages or antennae.
  • Failed molts (the new shell becomes fused to the old one at the infection site).

Quarantine & Prevention

  • Isolate Infected Shrimp: While not as fast-spreading as a virus, it is contagious.
  • Remove Molts: Pull any molts from the tank immediately, as they harbor the bacteria.
  • Improve Water Quality: High organic waste (nitrates) significantly speeds up the bacteria's growth.

Treatment

  • Salt Dips: 1 tablespoon of aquarium salt in 1 cup of tank water for 60 seconds once a day.
  • Hydrogen Peroxide (H2O2): 1.5ml of 3% H2O2 per gallon of tank water for 3-5 days.
  • Indian Almond Leaves: The natural tannins have antibacterial properties that help the shrimp fight the infection.
